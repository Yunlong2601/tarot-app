# Fix ShenTarot's trust leaks before selling harder

ShenTarot's biggest conversion problem for a hesitant free user is not price. It is trust. The prices are already at or below the category: US$6.99 a month against a North American median of US$9.99, and US$39.99 a year, exactly the median. The real problem is that the app spends credits without saying so, labels a paid action "free", promises "50 readings a day" while Premium actually gives 90 to 150 credits a month, and sends users who run out of credits straight to a generic paywall with no explanation. Each of these is a small code or copy fix, and each lands on the fears that research finds behind payment hesitation: unclear value, fear of a renewal trap, and abstract credits whose real cost is hidden. The plan below therefore puts about ten bug and copy fixes first (days of work, mostly strings in 15 locale files), then a context-aware out-of-credits sheet and paywall, then value-moment features: making the one premium-spread try genuinely free, previewing the look back, and a relationship spread. Pricing changes come last and only as tests, because the evidence on pricing is the weakest. A realistic target for a generous freemium app like this is about 2–3% of installs paying by day 35, with about 5% as a top-quartile stretch. Nearly all quantitative benchmarks here come from vendors that sell subscription tooling (RevenueCat, Adapty, Superwall), the strongest academic evidence on subscription traps is a working paper, and nothing has been measured on ShenTarot itself, so every target and lift below is a hypothesis to test, not a forecast.

## The hesitant user hits a silent paywall that leads with a 50-a-day promise

A read-only audit of the live configuration (credits on, Apple sign-in on) traced what a cautious newcomer actually sees. It was not checked at runtime, so layout and timing effects are unverified. Onboarding promises "Start with 10 free readings, then get a free one every day" ([onboarding_screen.dart:26-31](../app/lib/features/onboarding/onboarding_screen.dart)). The sign-in screen that follows sells sign-in only as a way to "keep Premium when you change or add a phone" and **never mentions the second daily credit**, which is the main reason a free user would sign in ([account_screen.dart:90-137](../app/lib/features/you/account_screen.dart)). From then on, the balance appears only as a 12-point grey line on the Today, Draw and Photo screens ([today_section.dart:37-47](../app/lib/features/guide/today_section.dart); [app_state.dart:110-141](../app/lib/app/app_state.dart)).

The audit found six places where a credit is spent, and **four of them never show the cost**: "Go deeper" on the Card of the Day, "Start reading" in the spread picker, "Get my reading" on the draw screen, and "Read" in the Look-first dialog. Only follow-ups ("Each question uses 1 credit") and the clarifier show a price ([daily_card_screen.dart:114-120](../app/lib/features/read/daily_card_screen.dart); [draw_screen.dart:323](../app/lib/features/draw/draw_screen.dart); [start_reading.dart:169-217](../app/lib/features/reading/start_reading.dart); [reading_screen.dart:281-303](../app/lib/features/reading/reading_screen.dart)). After a reading the balance refreshes silently, with no "1 credit used, 9 left" confirmation ([start_reading.dart:51](../app/lib/features/reading/start_reading.dart)). The one-time premium-spread try is labelled "Uses your one-time free premium reading" with a "Start free reading" button, yet **the server charges a credit for it** and refuses it at zero balance ([spread_picker_screen.dart:148-221](../app/lib/features/read/spread_picker_screen.dart); [backend index.ts:410-437](../backend/src/index.ts)).

When the balance is known to be zero, every reading button opens the paywall with no message at all ([app_state.dart:334-341](../app/lib/app/app_state.dart); [start_reading.dart:28-31](../app/lib/features/reading/start_reading.dart)). A well-written sign-in nudge exists ("Get a second free reading every day"), but on the credits path it is effectively unreachable. The backend sets its `guest` flag by comparing the limit with the guest daily allowance, and with the credit wallet on that comparison is always false ([index.ts:254-264, 436](../backend/src/index.ts); [start_reading.dart:121-138, 323-355](../app/lib/features/reading/start_reading.dart)). So the most common out-of-credits guest skips the free fix (sign in for 2 a day) and lands on a paywall whose first bullet reads **"All spreads, follow-ups, 50 readings a day"**. Premium actually gives 90, 120 or 150 credits a month depending on the plan, which is roughly 3–5 a day; 50 a day is only a spending cap ([paywall_screen.dart:174-183](../app/lib/features/paywall/paywall_screen.dart); [usage.ts:78-84](../backend/src/usage.ts)). The same outdated claim appears on the You-tab Premium card and on the Welcome-to-Premium screen ("Up to 50 readings a day — Read as often as you like"), and the Guide FAQ still describes "two AI readings a day" and follow-ups as Premium-only ([you_screen.dart:225-231](../app/lib/features/you/you_screen.dart); [welcome_premium_screen.dart:48-52](../app/lib/features/paywall/welcome_premium_screen.dart); [guide_screen.dart:72,82](../app/lib/features/guide/guide_screen.dart)).

The paywall itself has the elements the brief already noted. Annual is pre-selected with "$1.99 for your first month · just $3.33/month · save 52% vs monthly" and a "Start for $1.99" button. A terms line spells out the renewal. The trust line reads "No surprise charges: cancel anytime, and we can remind you 2 days before it ends", and the app offers that reminder after purchase. Below the plans sits a "Not ready to subscribe?" pack card ([paywall_screen.dart:236-341, 466-524](../app/lib/features/paywall/paywall_screen.dart); [app_state.dart:603-619](../app/lib/app/app_state.dart)). The paywall's gaps are structural. It does not know why the user arrived, it has no sign-in path, and the only complete explanation of credits is a five-sentence paragraph inside the pack card, which a user who never opens the paywall never sees.

Several smaller defects compound the impression of carelessness, which matters for a product whose credibility is already questioned because it is AI. They are listed in the table below.

## Hesitation is rational risk management, so transparency is the main ethical lever

Users who pause before paying are mostly not being irrational. The strongest modern evidence comes from a large field experiment at a European newspaper paywall. It measured **a 53–75% chance that subscribers fail to take an action they want within a month**, and found that consumers anticipate only about one-sixth of that inertia. Yet 24–36% of potential subscribers deliberately avoided auto-renewing offers, and 9% stayed away for two years after being shown one ([BFI working paper](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/working-paper/sophisticated-consumers-with-inertia-long-term-implications-from-a-large-scale-field-experiment/)). In the January 2026 draft, auto-renewal lowered subscription take-up by 35% and the total subscriber base by 23% over 20 months, although an earlier version reported smaller effects. This is a working paper, not yet peer-reviewed ([Wharton-hosted draft](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Strulov-Shlain-Avner-PAPER-Subscriptions.pdf)). For ShenTarot the implication is direct. A hesitant user looking at "Start for $1.99" is right to worry about the US$39.99 that follows, and making that path easy to leave is the way to win the users who are wary of traps.

Credits add a second, separate worry. Research on "medium maximization" shows that inserting points between effort and reward changes choices and creates "an illusion of advantage" ([Hsee et al. 2003, JCR](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v30y2003i1p1-14.html)). Less visible forms of payment are also spent more freely ([APA on Raghubir & Srivastava 2008](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2008/09/credit-cash)). Regulators have drawn the obvious conclusion. The EU's consumer-protection authorities (the CPC Network) issued non-binding principles in March 2025 that treat in-app currencies used mainly for payment as money, and ask for clear real-currency prices with no forced excess purchases ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/qas-on-the-eu-consumer-protection-authorities-joint-guidance-paper/)). There is a real tension here. Mental-accounting research predicts that a visible per-use price creates a "taxi-meter" effect that makes consumption less enjoyable ([Prelec & Loewenstein 1998](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.17.1.4)), and flat-rate plans appeal partly because they let people avoid watching the meter run ([Lambrecht & Skiera 2006](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.2.212)). The resolution this report recommends is to show the cost of each action plainly but quietly ("1 credit · 9 left"), and to sell Premium honestly as the way to stop counting, not to hide the meter. Hiding cost might lift short-term spending, but it is exactly what the CPC principles and the FTC's dark-patterns report target, and it produces the credit-loss complaints seen in competitor reviews.

The third worry is that the reading is "just AI". Sixteen preregistered experiments (N = 27,491) found a persistent penalty when creative writing is disclosed as AI-generated, driven by lower perceived authenticity ([Raj, Berg & Seamans](https://citations.prolific.com/paper/the-artificial-intelligence-disclosure-penalty-humans-persistently-devalue-ai-generated-creative-wri)). A separate experiment found that willingness to pay fell when a book was attributed to AI ([NIM](https://www.nim.org/publikationen/detail/transparency-without-trust)). No study tests this on AI interpretation or advice specifically, so its transfer to tarot is an inference. The ethical answer is not to hide the AI. It is to make the personal inputs visible: the user's own scanned cards, their question, what they noticed in the Look-first step. People value what they have invested effort in (the [IKEA effect](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)) and what they already own ([endowment](https://doi.org/10.1086/261737)).

The levers that work without manipulation all make value felt before the ask. Curiosity rises when people notice a gap in their knowledge ([Loewenstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)), which supports genuine previews. Endowed progress raises completion when the progress is real ([Nunes & Drèze 2006](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480)). A "free" option exerts outsized pull ([Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely 2007](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1060.0254)), which is why the daily free credit is worth protecting, and also why using the word "free" for a paid action is so damaging. On the trust side, Blinkist redesigned its paywall around a plain trial timeline with a reminder and reported **+23% trial sign-ups and −55% customer-service complaints** ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/how-solving-our-biggest-customer-complaint-at-blinkist-led-to-a-23-increase-in-conversion-b60ad514134b)). That is one company's self-reported case without a published control, but it points the same way as the Miller et al. field data. ShenTarot already has the reminder and a trust line. What it lacks is the timeline, the visible cost at each spend point, and an honest, friendly message when credits run out.

## Competitors charge more, and users punish trial traps and clawed-back freebies

Against its category, ShenTarot is not expensive. Labyrinthos, the closest tarot comparable, sells credits from $0.99, Premium "Unlimited Personalized Readings" at $9.99, a quarterly plan at $24.99 and an $89.99 tier that is probably annual ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1155180220)). Co-Star's Pro-Star is $8.99, and its question packs run 5 for $2.99, 10 for $4.99 and 25 for $6.99 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/co-star-personalized-astrology/id1264782561)). AI tarot apps cluster at $9.99 a month and $49.99–59.99 a year, such as Arcara at $9.99 and $59.99 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/arcara-ai-tarot-reading/id6757468022)), and many sell weekly plans at $5.99–6.99 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ai-tarot-card-reading/id6450309122)). One-time options are common: Moonly Lifetime Pro at $39.99 ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/paywall-library/moonly/)) and Tarotale full access at $24.99 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tarotale-ai-tarot-reading/id6739936019)). ShenTarot's $6.99 monthly and $39.99 annual prices sit at the low end. That leaves headroom later, but it also means the price is not what blocks a hesitant user. No competitor seen scans physical cards. Labyrinthos and Golden Thread only let users log physical-card readings by hand, so the scan is a real point of difference for people who own a deck.

Negative reviews about paying cluster around four themes, and ShenTarot can avoid all of them. The first is clawing back free value. A Co-Star reviewer called "$9.00 a month for an ai astrology service" insane and said they would return if the free daily features came back ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/co-star-personalized-astrology/id1264782561)). The second is trial-to-weekly conversions that were poorly disclosed, with reminders arriving after the charge, as reported for Nebula. The third is buried cancellation flows and charges after cancelling, as reported for The Pattern; both of these come from a single commercial 2026 summary of reviews and are unverified ([Unstar](https://unstar.app/blog/co-star-sanctuary-pattern-nebula-stellium-astrology-apps-ranked-2026)). The fourth is losing credits to errors ([App Store, AI Tarot: Reading Chat](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ai-tarot-reading-chat/id6449005723)). Positive reviews reward the opposite. Labyrinthos users call the prices "incredibly fair" and value its free readings, and a Nebula reviewer accepted the price because a personal astrologer "would cost a fortune." The evidence base is thin: three or four visible reviews per App Store page plus one third-party summary, with no systematic sentiment counts. Even so, the pattern is consistent enough to act on. Keep the daily free credit permanent, never take a credit for a failed or refused reading, and price against the cost of a human reading rather than against nothing.

What competitors gate also shows where paying feels natural. Relationship or "about another person" readings are the most consistently monetized moment (Co-Star's friend charts, The Pattern's Connect products, Nebula's $4.99 Relationship Check-In). Follow-up questions come next (Co-Star question packs, Arcara's unlimited follow-ups), then deeper spreads and saved history ([App Store, TarotAI](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tarotai-daily-card-readings/id6447095993)). Daily cards are the free hook everywhere. ShenTarot already gates follow-ups and long spreads well. Its gaps are a relationship spread and a preview of its retention features (the look back and the monthly recap). No public data ranks these moments by actual conversion; the ranking is inferred from what competitors choose to gate.

For a generous freemium app, a sensible starting target is **2–3% of installs making any purchase (pack or subscription) by D35**, with **about 5% as a stretch**. The hard-paywall 10–12% does not apply unless the free tier is cut, and cutting it would trigger the backlash competitors see. Two ShenTarot-specific targets matter more than the headline rate. The first is the share of $1.99 intro subscribers who reach the full-price renewal; the single case study suggests planning around 40–50%, and 35% of annual cancellations land in month one anyway. The second is refunds on Premium below about 4%, which removing the "50 a day" overpromise should help. Finally, because 82–89% of trials start on install day while ShenTarot's 11 starting credits push the first "out of credits" moment to day 3–6 for a typical reader, the first-session experience and the first out-of-credits moment are separate funnels and should be measured separately. If the app does not already log paywall views by entry reason, that instrumentation is a prerequisite for every test below.

Two candidate changes are deliberately left off the list. The first is cutting welcome credits to force an earlier limit. The day-0 purchase data argues for it, but the Co-Star backlash and the zero-price effect argue against it, and Tier 1 makes the current limit honest first. If tested at all, test 10 against 5 welcome credits with the out-of-credits sheet in place, and keep the daily credit untouched. The second is an onboarding paywall. A soft, dismissible Premium offer right after the first reading is compatible with the evidence that most conversions happen on day 0. A paywall before the first reading would ask a hesitant user to pay before they have seen any value, and hard-paywall refunds are 70% higher (5.8% vs 3.4%).

## Pricing: keep the ladder, show the price per reading, and test before raising

Per credit, the current ladder is steep. That is fine, but it is invisible to the user.

| Option | Price | Credits | Cost per credit | Also includes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pack S | $1.99 | 10 | 19.9¢ | — |
| Pack M | $3.99 | 25 | 16.0¢ | — |
| Pack L | $5.99 | 40 | 15.0¢ | — |
| Monthly | $6.99/mo | 90/mo | 7.8¢ | 5/7/10-card spreads, look back, recap |
| 3-month | $14.99 | 120/mo (360) | 4.2¢ | same |
| Annual | $39.99/yr ($1.99 first month) | 150/mo (1,800) | 2.2¢ | same |

**Packs against monthly.** The $5.99 pack sits $1 below a month of Premium that gives more than twice the credits plus the long spreads. For anyone who would actually use 40 credits, that makes the pack look dominated by monthly. This overlap is mostly a feature, not a bug. The pack's real customer is the person who will not accept auto-renewal at any price (the "sophisticated" avoiders in Miller et al.), and for everyone else it works as an honest anchor for Premium. Recommendation: keep all three packs, show the per-reading price (change 14), and track two numbers, the share of first purchases that are packs and the pack-to-subscriber rate. Revisit only if packs take most revenue from users who read daily. No public data exists on whether credit packs cannibalize subscriptions; only about 10.7% of apps run subscriptions plus consumables ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)), so this is genuinely unknown.

**The annual discount** is already deep: $39.99 against $83.88 for twelve monthly payments, a 52% saving. **Do not deepen it.** If anything, the anchor is weak because the monthly price is low ($6.99 against a $9.99 median), not because the annual is high. One case study found yearly conversion unchanged between $40 and $60 ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/introductory-offers-apps)), and higher-priced apps convert more installs, not fewer (2.8% against 1.4% at D35 ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps))). That suggests room to test $49.99 annual or $7.99–9.99 monthly. But it is correlational, it comes from one app, and it should wait until Tier 1 and 2 have fixed the trust problems, or the test will measure the broken copy instead of the price. Keep the $1.99 first month for now and judge it by month-2 renewal. Paid intros screen for intent: web-publisher data shows $1 trials retaining 82% at trial end against 62% for free trials ([Spiny on Piano](https://trends.spiny.ai/?p=1160)), although that is news websites, not apps. A later A/B test against a 7-day free trial is reasonable, since trials of 5–9 days convert at 37.4% against 25.5% for trials of 4 days or less ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)).

**The 3-month plan** ($5.00 a month, 28% off) has no published benchmark at all. It may help as a middle option that makes annual look like the obvious pick, or it may split attention. Adapty associates three-plan paywalls with the highest LTV, but its three plans are weekly, monthly and annual ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-2025-in-10-minutes/)). This is low priority: test a two-plan paywall (annual and monthly) only once traffic supports it.

**Lifetime and one-time options.** Some users clearly want a path with no subscription. Lifetime is common: 23.2% of apps pair subscriptions with lifetime purchases ([RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)), and Moonly sells Lifetime Pro at $39.99. But an unlimited-AI lifetime product is dangerous, because every reading has a real model cost, and RevenueCat warns that AI apps carry real COGS ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/ai-hybrid-monetization)). The safer one-time product is a **permanent unlock of the 5, 7 and 10-card spreads** (for example $14.99–19.99) that still spends credits per reading. That separates access from consumption, which is the hybrid principle. Treat this as a hypothesis worth one test, not a recommendation backed by data. **Do not add weekly plans.** They are the main source of trap complaints in the category and keep only 3.4% of subscribers after a year. **Do** use Apple's localized price tiers for lower-income storefronts, since willingness to pay varies 2–3x by country ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-2025-in-10-minutes/)).

## Lines not to cross: Apple 3.1.x, the FTC and EU law

Apple's rules already forbid most of what a growth playbook might tempt the team toward. Purchased credits "may not expire" (3.1.1). Subscriptions sold "under false pretenses" or by "bait-and-switch" lead to removal (3.1.2(a)). Before asking for a subscription the app must "clearly describe what the user will get for the price" (3.1.2(c)), which is why the "50 readings a day" copy is a compliance issue and not only a style issue. Promoting services that are not offered is grounds for removal (2.3.1), and apps cannot force reviews (3.2.2(x)) ([Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Apple's subscription guidance also requires the billed amount to be the most prominent price, with per-month equivalents such as "$3.33/month" in a "subordinate position and size" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/subscriptions/)). With an intro offer, the user is billed first $1.99 and then $39.99, so the $39.99 renewal must stay plainly visible next to the "Start for $1.99" button. Superwall's own case study warns that "transaction-abandon" discount popups can cause App Review problems as "unintentional purchases" ([Superwall](https://superwall.com/blog/17-revenue-boost-with-transaction-abandon-paywalls-a-case-study)). Guideline 5.6 was not captured in the research and should be read directly before launch.

**In the US**, the FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit on **8 July 2025** on procedural grounds ([Benesch](https://www.beneschlaw.com/publication/eighth-circuit-cancels-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule/)). The FTC restarted rulemaking with an advance notice of proposed rulemaking announced on **11 March 2026** and continues to enforce ROSCA and Section 5 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/)). Whether a proposed rule has followed is unconfirmed. State auto-renewal laws such as California's remain in force ([Lowenstein Sandler](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/ftc-click-to-cancel-rule-on-hold-but-california-s-automatic-renewal-law-remains-intact-what-companies-need-to-know-privacy)). **In the EU**, the online "withdrawal button" under Directive 2023/2673 has applied since **19 June 2026** ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/eu-consumer-law-new-withdrawal-button-requirements-for-online-contracts)). How it interacts with Apple-processed purchases, where Apple is the merchant of record, needs legal advice. The CPC virtual-currency principles ask for real-currency pricing of credits. The Digital Fairness Act, aimed at subscription traps and dark patterns, had no confirmed Commission proposal as of May 2026 ([Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html)), and the consumer group BEUC is lobbying to ban premium virtual currencies outright ([BEUC](https://www.beuc.eu/sites/default/files/publications/BEUC-X-2026-019_Checklist_Towards_the_Digital_Fairness_Act.pdf)). Designing to the strictest of these standards costs ShenTarot almost nothing, because its model is already close.

Concretely, ShenTarot should not do any of the following:
- fake countdowns or recurring "today only" offers;
- confirm-shaming dismiss buttons ("No, I don't want clarity");
- a delayed or hidden close button;
- expiring or "bonus" paid credits;
- pack sizes engineered to leave stranded balances;
- fabricated reviews or "X people reading now";
- discount popups when a user abandons the purchase sheet;
- cutting the daily free credit after users rely on it;
- claims about prediction accuracy or life outcomes, which would contradict the entertainment-and-reflection positioning;
- calling anything "free" that spends a credit.

The app must also not promise its own money-back guarantee. Refunds for in-app purchases go through Apple, and the honest move is to explain how to request one.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "how do we persuade hesitant users to pay?" to "where is ShenTarot accidentally confirming their suspicions?". Each of the user's three rational fears has a specific trigger in the code. The fear that the product is not worth it is fed by the never-previewed look back and a "free" try that is not free. The fear of being trapped is answered only partly, by a reminder that exists but no timeline. The fear of not knowing the real cost is fed by four silent spend points and a 50-a-day claim. Fixing these is cheap, and much of it is also required for compliance. That reverses the usual order of growth work: here the ethical fixes are the highest-return experiments, and pricing, the lever teams usually reach for first, is the one with the least evidence and the most to lose if tested before trust is repaired.

The deeper strategic point is that ShenTarot's hybrid model is an asset for exactly this audience. A $1.99 no-renewal pack and a permanent daily free credit give the cautious user a way to say "not yet" without leaving, which the field evidence suggests keeps a larger long-run customer base than pushing everyone into auto-renewal. Treat each pack buyer as a future subscriber who has not yet been shown why. Measure the path from pack to Premium, from the free premium try to Premium, and from the out-of-credits sheet onward, and let those ShenTarot-specific numbers replace the vendor benchmarks in this report within one or two release cycles.
